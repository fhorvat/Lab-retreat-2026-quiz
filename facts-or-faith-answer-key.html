Q · 01
Cell biology · stress response

When cells are stressed, ribosomes are protected by forming inactive ribosome dimers.

Facts Stress-induced ribosome dimerisation is a conserved, structurally characterised mechanism for preserving translation machinery during nutrient starvation and other insults.

The 2024 cryo-EM structure of the stress-induced mammalian 100S-like dimer showed how two 80S ribosomes inactivate each other at their decoding centres, preserving the pool for rapid recovery once stress is relieved. Bacterial 100S particles have been known for longer.

ReferenceKarki et al., Science 384 (2024), doi:10.1126/science.adr4287 — science.org/doi/full/10.1126/science.adr4287

Q · 02
Developmental biology · allometry

Across placental mammals, mean oocyte diameter scales with adult body mass with an allometric exponent of ~0.1 — a human oocyte is ~110 µm, an African elephant oocyte ~145 µm, and a fin whale oocyte ~180 µm.

Faith The allometric framing and specific micrometre values sound authoritative, but mammalian oocyte size is essentially decoupled from body mass. The cited numbers are not in the literature.

Mammalian ovulated oocytes cluster around 70–120 µm in diameter across nearly four orders of magnitude of adult body mass. Mouse, human, cow, elephant, and whale oocytes are all within a factor of ~2 — there is no robust allometric scaling. The decoupling of egg size from body size is in fact one of the defining features of placental reproduction (compare with birds, where egg mass does scale with body mass). The exponent and species-specific values in the question are fabricated to sound rigorous; this is a useful illustration of how easily quantitative dressing can launder an untrue claim.

ReferenceGriffin, Emery & Huggins, Reproduction 132 (2006), 191–206 — review of mammalian oocyte size across species.

Q · 03
Genome biology · endogenous retroviruses

Roughly 8% of the human genome consists of DNA left behind by ancient retroviruses that infected our ancestors.

Facts Human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs) and their remnants make up about 8% of our nuclear DNA — a permanent genetic fossil record of past germ-line infections.

When a retrovirus integrates into a germ-line cell, the provirus can be inherited by all descendants. Over tens of millions of years this happened repeatedly, leaving HERVs and their solo long-terminal-repeats across the genome — several times more sequence than all protein-coding exons combined (~1.5%). Most copies are inactivated by mutation, but some retain functional elements that the host has domesticated, including the placental fusion protein syncytin, which derives from a retroviral envelope gene.

ReferencesInternational Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (Lander et al.), Nature 409 (2001), 860–921 — ~8% of the genome is retroviral-derived.
Bannert & Kurth, PNAS 101 (2004), 14572–14579 — review of HERVs.

Q · 04
Behaviour · evolutionary psychology

The facial width-to-height ratio (fWHR) of adult human males is a reliable signature of aggressive behaviour.

Faith A widely cited but increasingly contested claim. Original effects do not survive pre-registration, larger samples, or controls for body size and sex.

Early reports (Carré & McCormick, 2008) found fWHR correlated with penalty minutes in hockey players. Subsequent meta-analyses and pre-registered replications (e.g., Kosinski 2017; Wang et al., 2019; Caton et al., 2022) report effect sizes near zero once confounds — body mass, age, photo quality, observer rater — are properly controlled. The sexual-dimorphism premise has also been challenged: adult fWHR is not reliably larger in men than women.

ReferencesKosinski, Psych. Sci. 28 (2017), 1675–1682 — large-sample non-replication.
Caton, Hannan & Dixson, Psychol. Bull. 148 (2022), 33–60 — meta-analysis.

Q · 05
Physiology · metabolism

Healthy adult humans retain deposits of metabolically active brown fat that burn energy to generate heat — tissue long thought to disappear after infancy.

Facts PET–CT and biopsy studies confirmed depots of functional, cold-activated brown adipose tissue in adults, burning glucose and fat to produce heat.

Brown adipose tissue (BAT) dissipates chemical energy as heat through the mitochondrial uncoupling protein UCP1. It was long believed to vanish after infancy, but in 2009 several groups independently used 18F-FDG PET–CT to image cold-activated, UCP1-positive BAT in the supraclavicular and paravertebral regions of healthy adults. BAT mass and activity decline with age and obesity and are inversely correlated with BMI, making the tissue a target for metabolic-disease research.

ReferencesCypess et al., N. Engl. J. Med. 360 (2009), 1509–1517.
van Marken Lichtenbelt et al., NEJM 360 (2009), 1500–1508.
Virtanen et al., NEJM 360 (2009), 1518–1525.

Q · 06
Neuroscience · organelle traffic

In the adult mammalian brain, healthy neurons routinely donate mitochondria to damaged neighbours through tunneling nanotubes, restoring metabolic function.

Faith Tunneling nanotubes are real in culture; routine, functionally rescuing mitochondrial transfer between adult neurons in vivo is not established.

TNTs were described by Rustom et al. (2004) and mitochondrial transfer via TNTs has been demonstrated in cell-culture systems and between astrocytes and neurons in stroke models. The leap to "routinely, between neurons, in the intact adult brain, as a rescue mechanism" is not supported: direct in vivo imaging of neuron-to-neuron TNT-mediated mitochondrial transfer in mammals is lacking, and the structures described in vivo are often blebs or filopodia rather than bona fide TNTs. The claim sits comfortably in the gap between "biologically possible" and "demonstrated".

ReferencesRustom et al., Science 303 (2004), 1007–1010 — original TNT description.
Hayakawa et al., Nature 535 (2016), 551–555 — astrocyte→neuron mitochondrial transfer in stroke (not TNT-mediated; extracellular vesicles).
Cordero Cervantes & Zurzolo, EMBO J. 40 (2021), e105789 — review of what is and is not established about TNTs in the nervous system.

Q · 07
Human genetics · disease resistance

A natural mutation in the CCR5 gene (Δ32) makes people who inherit two copies almost completely resistant to infection by the common strain of HIV.

Facts Homozygous CCR5-Δ32 carriers lack a working CCR5 co-receptor, which most HIV-1 strains need to enter cells, and are highly resistant to infection.

CCR5 is the chemokine co-receptor that R5-tropic HIV-1 uses, alongside CD4, to enter T cells. The Δ32 allele is a 32-bp deletion producing a truncated receptor that never reaches the cell surface. Individuals homozygous for Δ32 (~1% of people of European ancestry) are strongly protected against R5-strain infection; heterozygotes progress to AIDS more slowly. This is the basis of the only confirmed HIV cures to date — the "Berlin" and "London" patients, who received bone-marrow transplants from Δ32/Δ32 donors.

ReferencesSamson et al., Nature 382 (1996), 722–725.
Liu et al., Cell 86 (1996), 367–377 — Δ32 homozygotes resist HIV-1 infection.
Hütter et al., NEJM 360 (2009), 692–698 — the Berlin patient.

Q · 08
Neuroscience · adult plasticity

Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is a robust, lifelong phenomenon in humans and contributes substantively to learning and memory.

Faith A textbook claim that is much less settled than it sounds. The most stringent recent studies find that human dentate-gyrus neurogenesis drops sharply in childhood and is undetectable in adults; the functional contribution to memory in humans has never been directly shown.

Adult neurogenesis is well established in rodents. Extending it to humans rests on much shakier ground. Sorrells et al. (Nature, 2018) examined 59 postmortem and surgical samples and found doublecortin⁺ / PSA-NCAM⁺ neuroblasts essentially absent after age ~13. Moreno-Jiménez et al. (2019) and Boldrini et al. (2018) reported the opposite using different tissue-fixation protocols — and the debate has not been resolved; current consensus is "uncertain, possibly very low rate." Critically, even the studies that argue neurogenesis persists do not demonstrate a causal contribution to memory in humans. The leap from "some new neurons may exist" to "robust, lifelong, substantively contributing to learning" is the faith part.

ReferencesSorrells et al., Nature 555 (2018), 377–381 — neurogenesis "drops to undetectable levels in adults".
Moreno-Jiménez et al., Nat. Med. 25 (2019), 554–560 — counter-evidence, fixation-dependent.
Kempermann et al., Cell Stem Cell 23 (2018), 25–30 — joint statement: rate, if non-zero, is far below rodent levels and functional role in humans is unproven.

Q · 09
Reproductive biology · microchimerism

Cells from a fetus can cross into the mother during pregnancy and persist in her tissues — including her brain — for decades afterward.

Facts Fetal cells routinely enter the maternal circulation and engraft in maternal organs; male (Y-chromosome) DNA has been detected in women decades after pregnancy, including in post-mortem brain.

Cells traffic in both directions across the placenta, so a mother acquires a small population of genetically distinct fetal cells, and the fetus acquires maternal cells — fetomaternal microchimerism. These cells can persist for decades; a standard assay detects male DNA in the blood (and tissues) of women who have borne sons. Microchimeric cells have been found integrated in maternal organs and are being investigated for roles in wound healing, autoimmunity, and cancer. The striking part — persistence in the maternal brain — has been reported directly.

ReferencesBianchi et al., PNAS 93 (1996), 705–708 — fetal cells persist in maternal blood for years.
Chan et al., PLoS ONE 7 (2012), e45592 — male microchimerism in the female human brain.

Q · 10
Microbiome · behaviour

Higher faecal abundance of Prevotella copri in adults is associated with elevated trait neuroticism, with an effect size (β ≈ 0.18) comparable to known psychosocial predictors.

Faith The specific taxon, the standardised coefficient, and the comparison to "known psychosocial predictors" all sound like a real abstract — but the specific P. copri–neuroticism claim does not survive replication or covariate adjustment. The numerical effect size in the question is fabricated.

There are published associations between gut taxa and personality scales (e.g., Johnson 2020 reports correlations with Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella), and trait neuroticism has been a popular outcome. The robustness gap is large: most associations are derived in single cohorts of n < 1000, do not adjust adequately for diet, BMI, antibiotic exposure, or recent stress, rarely replicate out-of-sample, and the reported standardised effects shrink towards zero in pooled analyses. Mendelian-randomisation studies aiming to establish causal direction yield mostly null results. The lesson: a specific taxon name plus a credible-looking β is exactly how an unreplicated single-cohort finding ends up cited as established biology.

ReferencesJohnson, Hum. Microbiome J. 15 (2020), 100069 — exploratory associations.
Hughes et al., Nat. Microbiol. 5 (2020), 1079–1087 — twin-cohort analyses showing diet dominates microbiome variance.
Liu et al., Nat. Genet. 54 (2022), 52–61 — MR analysis: limited evidence for causal personality→microbiome or microbiome→personality links.

Q · 11
Animal behaviour · locomotion

On steep, slippery slopes, penguins observe a right-of-way rule in which birds climbing uphill are given priority over those heading down.

Faith An appealing, orderly-sounding rule — but there is no peer-reviewed evidence that penguins follow an uphill right-of-way convention. Movement on slopes is better explained by individual footing and following established trails.

Penguins do form well-worn paths ("penguin highways"), travel in single file, and there is genuine work on the biomechanics of their waddling gait and on the coordinated, traffic-like dynamics of huddles. But a formal social rule granting uphill climbers priority over descenders has not been documented in the behavioural-ecology literature. The claim borrows the plausibility of human traffic norms and of real penguin trail-following to dress up a convention that, as stated, lacks empirical support — a good example of an "of course animals would do that" intuition with no study behind it. (If you have a source for a genuine uphill-priority rule, this item can flip to Facts.)

ReferenceZitterbart et al., PLoS ONE 6 (2011), e20260 — traveling-wave dynamics of emperor-penguin huddles (real coordinated movement; not an uphill-priority rule). No peer-reviewed source supports the specific claim as stated.
